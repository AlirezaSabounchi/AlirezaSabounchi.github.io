It seems like AI is everywhere now. You cannot escape it no matter how hard you try (unless you decide to live off-grid in a forest, which may or may not dry up due to AI's overconsumption of water, among other resorces). But, for an AI enthusiast like myself, this is a good thing(?).

The rapid evolution of artificial intelligence is now at the heart of drug discovery. Most PhD positions offered in the field as of late, sqeeze AI/ML somewhere in their posting. Truth be told, some innovative deep learning algorithms are enabling researchers to predict protein–ligand interactions with unprecedented accuracy, (somewhat) streamlining the identification of potential therapeutic compounds. Surprislingly, this doesn't seem to translate into faster / more efficient drug discovery.

Recent breakthroughs in representation learning have indeed paved the way for the development of robust molecular representations. One promising approach is the integration of graph neural networks (on which I already made a post), which model the complex relationships within molecules more effectively than traditional methods (but maybe less so compared to the fancy transformers or LLMs). This allows for more precise predictions of binding affinities, ultimately improving the success rate of identifying viable drug candidates. Yet, when it comes to getting clinically meaningful results, these approaches do not seem to be working miracles, or maybe, are not trusted enough to be allowed to leave academia and enter the pharmaceutical industry.

The question is, when can we be certain that these approaches are good enough to become mainstream? Should we wait until their loss values reach a mathematically-verified lower bound, or can we utilize them in real-life senarios to see whether the proposed testing methods even measure how good a model can perfom compared to wet-lab experiments?

As these technologies continue to mature, they are destined to revolutionize pharmaceutical research, offering new hope in the fight against complex diseases.
